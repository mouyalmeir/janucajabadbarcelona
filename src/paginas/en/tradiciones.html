Chanukah traditions

A game born of courage, and coins that teach us to give.

The dreidel game (sevivon)

When King Antiochus banned the study of Torah, Jewish children would meet in secret to learn. If a Greek patrol came near, they hid their scrolls and took out spinning tops, pretending to play. In honour of their courage we play dreidel (from the Yiddish drei, “to spin”), a four-sided top with the Hebrew letters Nun (נ), Gimel (ג), Hei (ה) and Shin (ש), the initials of Nes Gadol Hayah Sham (“A great miracle happened there”).

You start with 10 coins and there are 10 in the pot. Spin the dreidel!

  • נNun · NishtNothing: you don’t add or take anything.
  • גGimel · GantzEverything: you take the whole pot.
  • הHei · HalbHalf: you take half of the pot.
  • שShin · ShtelPut in: you add one coin to the pot.

How do you play?

Each player puts the same number of chocolate coins, nuts or counters into a shared pot. Taking turns, each player spins the dreidel and does what the letter facing up says.

Did you know…?

In the Land of Israel dreidels have a Pe (פ) instead of the Shin: Nes Gadol Hayah Po, “a great miracle happened here”.

Chanukah gelt (Chanukah coins)

It is customary to give children money or chocolate coins (Chanukah gelt) during the festival. The word Chanukah shares its root with chinuch (“education”). The custom aims to encourage their Torah study and to teach them from a young age the value of tzedakah (charity and justice), encouraging them to set aside part of their money to help others.
